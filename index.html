Reference Audit

Paste your reference list, or open a draft. Each entry is looked up in Crossref and OpenAlex, and where those two hold no clear match, in Semantic Scholar, DataCite and Open Library. You see which references exist, which carry a wrong year, author or DOI, and which have been retracted. With a draft, you also see the citations in your text that have no entry in the list, and the entries your text never cites.

What the labels mean

Found
A record with this title exists, and its first author and year agree with your reference.
Check
A record exists, but something differs: the year, the first author, the DOI, or part of the title. Invented references often look like this, and so do honest typing errors.
Retracted
The work exists and has been retracted. Cite it only if you discuss the retraction.
No record
None of the indexes that were asked holds a matching record. The card names the indexes. Together they cover most journal articles, many preprints, data sets and books, and they still miss many reports, theses and works not written in English. Check these by hand.

The five indexes

Crossref and OpenAlex are asked first, because they hold most journal articles. An entry with no clear match there is then looked up in Open Library, which holds books, and in Semantic Scholar, which holds preprints and conference papers. A DOI or an arXiv number that Crossref does not know is looked up in DataCite, which registers preprints, data sets and software. Semantic Scholar limits how often it answers, so on some runs it is left out, and a card that says “No record” lists the indexes that did answer.

Opening a draft

You can open a Word file, a PDF or a text file. The page finds the section headed “References” or “Bibliography”, puts its entries in the box above, and keeps the rest of the text for one further check. That check reads the citations in your text, both the author and year kind such as (Merton, 1968) and the numbered kind such as [12], and compares them with the list. It reports citations with no entry in the list and entries with no citation in the text.

A Word file gives the cleanest list. A PDF holds lines of text with no mark for where one reference ends, so entries read from a PDF can be joined or split wrongly. Look through the box before you run the check. Citations placed in footnotes are not read.

Rewriting the list in a citation style

After a check you can have the list rewritten in APA, Harvard, MLA, Chicago, Vancouver or IEEE style. Each entry that matched a record with a DOI is written out again from that record by the formatting service of doi.org, so the details come from the publisher's record and not from your typing. Entries with no confirmed record are left as you typed them. An entry labelled “Check” is rewritten from the record too, so read it against your source before you keep it.

What happens to your text

This page has no server of its own. A draft that you open is read inside your browser. Only the entries of the reference list leave it: your browser sends each one straight to the public indexes named above, and for rewriting it sends the DOI of a matched record to doi.org. The body of your draft is never sent anywhere, and nothing you paste or open is stored or seen by this site.

Limits

The check tells you whether a work exists and whether its details agree with yours. It does not read the work, so it cannot tell you whether the work supports the sentence you cite it for. A label of “Found” means the reference is real, and the reading is still yours to do. The comparison with your text looks for a surname next to a year, so it can take a phrase such as “Paris 2015” for a citation.

Other ways to use it

A terminal script and a skill for AI coding agents such as Claude Code run the lookup in Crossref and OpenAlex on a text file or a BibTeX file and print a report. They are free for lists of any length. The instructions are on GitHub.
