How to find out whether a paper you cite has been retracted

A retraction is a public notice from a journal that a published article should no longer be relied on. The usual grounds are errors that undo the findings, data that cannot be verified, plagiarism, and fabricated results. The article stays online with the notice attached, so it is easy to cite one without knowing.

Why it matters

A retracted article cited as support weakens the argument that rests on it. An examiner or an editor who looks the article up sees the retraction notice at once.

How to check one paper by hand

  1. Open the article on the publisher's website through its DOI. A retracted article carries a notice at the top of the page, and its title often begins with the word RETRACTED.
  2. Search the Retraction Watch Database, the largest public list of retractions.
  3. If you keep your references in Zotero, look for the retraction warning on the item. Zotero compares your library with the Retraction Watch data and marks retracted items.

How to check a whole reference list

Reference Audit looks up every entry of a pasted list in Crossref, whose records carry the Retraction Watch data, and marks the retracted ones. It also reports references that have no record, and references whose year, first author or DOI differs from the record. Lists of up to 15 references are free, and no account is needed. Longer lists need a £5 pass that lasts 30 days.

When you may cite a retracted paper

You may cite it when the retraction is part of what you discuss, for example in a study of research misconduct. Say in the text that the article was retracted, and cite the retraction notice as well.
